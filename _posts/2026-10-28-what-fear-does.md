# What Fear Does When You Never Look at It

*On the worst case and the cost of avoidance*

Most of us carry a worst case around. Something we have thought about or felt, like a dark shape at the edge of the room that we so nicely and accurately arrange the furniture around. Ask someone to describe theirs and they will hesitate. They know it is bad. They would prefer not tell you what a day inside it looks like.

And that hesitation is where a knot lives.

## A picture

Seneca noticed this nearly two thousand years ago.

> There are more things, Lucilius, likely to frighten us than there are to crush us; we suffer more often in imagination than in reality.

*Seneca, Letters to Lucilius*

The imagined version of a disaster has absolutely no edges. But real hardship, even terrible hardship, is made of ordinary days, and ordinary days can be lived in. The picture in our head is missing exactly the details that make something survivable.
And most things are.
That's the odd thing about fear: it feels very clear and very certain, but when you look closely there's not much in it.

We just haven’t lived inside the picture we carry around, and that's a big part of why it scares us.

## Why avoiding it feeds it

Every time we decide to arrange a life to stay clear of the feared thing, the fear gets some kind of relief. The mind treats that relief as proof that the danger was real, and our avoidance worked. In doing so, we never collect the one piece of evidence that could correct the picture, which is what actually happens when we go near it or play with it.

This is why avoidance feels so much like safety but behaves clearly like a leash: your fear never has the chance to shrink, because nothing is ever allowed to test it. So we stay in a kind of limbo, feeding our evergrowing anxiety. More and more untested and unresolved fears.
Then our lives start becoming a shadow of what they used to be. And we call it aging or being wise.
And it all feels so reasonable while it's happening, which makes it hard to see.
Nobody wakes up and decides to make their life smaller. But it happens anyway, when we live our days around our fears calling the whole thing comfort.

The clinicians who treat anxiety build much of their work on the opposite move, gradual exposure to the feared thing in small and survivable doses. Cognitive therapy certainly borrows from the Stoics. Seneca's practice, a time of chosen simplicity to see what it is really like, is an ancient take of the same idea.

## What years of avoiding it will do

The cost of never looking at the worst case arrives as a life shrinking. You eventually set up your life so that nothing can go wrong. That means turning down the thing that might look foolish, and then the thing you could not undo. Each of those decisions seems logical, and each one is paid for in something you never see it is leaving you.

Over enough years the life gets smaller to fit your fears. Ironically, the fear itself stays exactly the same size, because it never met anything real. What changes is you. You become exasperatingly nervous of everything, and joy and confidence go first, since both need a little risk to come alive. You might still have what you need, yes, but you can no longer find what you want and any aliveness in your life.
You may know someone like this. Everything is in order, nothing is wrong, and still they seem a bit switched off or flat. 
Probably creativity is also lacking.
That is the price of our ‘worst case’ we never look at: a slow loss of colour.

## Rehearsing is not ruminating

It is easy to confuse this with worrying about situations, but they are nearly opposites. Worry circles. It asks what if and never answers. 
The Stoic rehearsal, which later writers called the premeditation of evils, asks one question and KEEPS answering it. 
And then what? Where would I sleep? Who would I call? What would I still have?

Try it with something small first. Keep asking « and then what » until you run out of things to imagine. Most people run out sooner than they expect. Somewhere in there you find ordinary things and you realise it's just another ordinary (or extraordinary) life experience.


Let's not confuse safety with life, or security with being alive.
