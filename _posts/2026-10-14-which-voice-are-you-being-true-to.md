*On the slogan we use to excuse almost anything*

"Just be yourself."

"I'm only being authentic."

"I'm being true to myself."

These sentences are everywhere, and they sound like freedom. Nobody wants to argue with them. But they rest on an assumption almost nobody examines: that we already know who "myself" is.

Most of us don't. We take the personality we were shaped into for the self, and we claim everything that moves through us as ours. A thought arrives, a craving, a sudden urge to withdraw or to attack, and it is stamped "mine" before we have looked at it. And if it is mine, then acting on it is authenticity.

Run that logic for a minute and see where it goes. A trauma response becomes authenticity. A narcissistic outburst becomes authenticity. Projecting my shadow onto you becomes me being honest. A belief absorbed from my family, my culture or my religion before I could question it becomes my truth. Even a desire that was never mine at all becomes who I really am.

## Whose voice is it

Marion Woodman, the Jungian analyst, said there is no sense talking about being true to yourself

> until you are sure what voice you are being true to.

*Marion Woodman*

She also said it takes hard work to tell the voices of the unconscious apart. In fact, there are many voices inside us. The wounded child speaks in the first person. So does the shame, the social program, the part that learned to survive.

Robert A. Johnson, who trained in the Jungian tradition and also spent time at the Sri Aurobindo Ashram, treated the word differentiation as something close to sacred. For him it was not intellectual discernment. It pointed somewhere much deeper, and it sits at the foundation of what "know thyself" actually asks of us.

## It does not fit in a list

This is why it frustrates me when inner work gets packaged into a short post with ten points. There is no list that tells you, this is my shadow, this is an inner child part, this is something else entirely. Frameworks can orient us but cannot discern for us.

Differentiation grows slowly, through years of obsessive self-observation. Radical honesty. Shadow work and inner child work. Paying attention to the body. Meditation. Psychological maturity. And the repeated, uncomfortable return to whatever we would rather not see in ourselves.

We also tend to go about this by adding. Another practice, another teacher, another framework, another layer of identity to wear. I feel it is much easier to find out who we are by removing. Every belief we see was handed to us, every role we stop performing, every desire we realise was never ours, takes something away. And what remains gets easier to recognise.

Over time something changes. We get familiar with the texture of our own psyche, the way you learn the sound of a voice you hear every day. We begin to feel, in the body and not in the head, the difference between an abandonment wound and a shame complex, between a trauma response and a belief we inherited, between a projection and a genuine perception. And behind all of it, quietly, something that was never any of those things.

None of this means becoming suspicious of every thought. It means loosening the reflex that says "I" and "mine" to everything that passes through.

## The sword over our era

Our era has its own sword of Damocles, and it is the endless freedom of choice. Everyone is told they are free, and everyone is choosing, all day, whatever they like. But if the one choosing is a bundle of conditioning, wounds and borrowed desires, then choice is just how the puppet moves. People feel free precisely because they are always choosing, and the more options there are, the less anyone stops to ask who is choosing.
Choice only means something once we know who is doing the choosing.

## The danger of hurry

None of this can be rushed, and the impatient ego hates that. 

This is also why pop-spiritual teachings, and especially a lot of channeled material, are so appealing right now. They are fast, they flatter, and they fit a short attention span. They let us feel advanced without the confrontation that would make us so. Instead of supporting real development they feed spiritual bypassing, and the seeker ends up overestimating their own level of being and wandering a maze they believe is a path.

I don't think "be yourself" is a wrong sentence. I think it is an unfinished one. It needs a question in front of it: which self? I would rather keep asking that for the rest of my life than answer it too quickly and end up with someone I am not.
