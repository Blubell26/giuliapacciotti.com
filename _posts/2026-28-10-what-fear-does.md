# What Fear Does When You Never Look at It

*On Seneca, the worst case, and the quiet cost of avoidance*

Most of us carry a worst case around. Not one we have examined, just one we have felt, a dark shape at the edge of the room that we arrange the furniture around. Ask someone to describe theirs precisely and they usually hesitate. They know it is bad. They could not tell you what Tuesday looks like inside it.

That hesitation is worth paying attention to.

## A picture, not a plan

Seneca noticed this nearly two thousand years ago.

> There are more things, Lucilius, likely to frighten us than there are to crush us; we suffer more often in imagination than in reality.

*Seneca, Letters to Lucilius*

The imagined version of a disaster has no edges. It has no hours in it, no logistics, no people who turn up, no small decisions to make. It is a feeling wearing the costume of a scene. Real hardship, even terrible hardship, is made of ordinary days, and ordinary days can be lived in. The picture in our head is missing exactly the details that make a thing survivable.

## Why avoiding it feeds it

Here is the quiet mechanism. Every time we arrange a life to stay clear of the feared thing, the fear gets relief. The mind files that relief as proof: the danger was real, and the avoidance worked. We never collect the one piece of evidence that could correct the picture, which is what actually happens when we go near it.

This is why avoidance feels like safety and behaves like a leash. The fear does not shrink, because nothing is allowed to test it. It just gets a larger territory to guard.

Clinicians who treat anxiety build much of their work on the opposite move, gradual exposure to the feared thing in small, survivable doses. Cognitive therapy openly borrows from the Stoics. Seneca's practice, a few days of chosen simplicity to see what it is really like, is an ancient cousin of the same idea.

## What years of avoiding it will do

The cost of never looking at the worst case does not arrive as a crisis. It arrives as a shrinking. You turn down the thing that might go wrong, then the thing that might look foolish, then the thing you could not undo. Each refusal is small and sensible, and each one is paid for in something you never see leave.

Over enough years the life gets smaller to fit the fear. The fear itself stays exactly the same size, because it never met anything real. What changes is you. You become quietly nervous of everything, and joy and confidence go first, since both need a little risk to breathe. You still have what you need, and you can no longer find what you want.

That is the real price of the worst case we never look at. Not the disaster itself, but the slow loss of colour.

## Rehearsing is not ruminating

It is easy to confuse this with worrying, and they are nearly opposites. Worry circles. It asks what if, and never answers. The Stoic rehearsal, which later writers called the premeditation of evils, asks one question and keeps answering it. And then what? Where would I sleep? Who would I call? What would I still have?

Specifics shrink things. And the answers tend to surprise us, because most of what we dread losing is a role, a position, a version of how we are seen. What survives, our skills, our people, our ability to adapt, turns out to be larger than the fear assumed. Psychologists have a name for part of this. We reliably predict that we will feel worse for longer after bad events than we actually do. The fear is not only vague, it is also exaggerated.

## Where this stops working

I want to be honest about the limits, because this can curdle into a slogan.

A few days of chosen simplicity is an exercise with a door you can open. Poverty or loss that is forced on someone is a different thing, and it would be false to pretend the first teaches you much about the second. And not every fear is fog. Some fear is information, about a real danger that deserves respect. The skill, and it is a lifelong one, is telling them apart. Seneca's practice is for the second kind, the fear of losing comfort, status, approval or certainty, which pretends to be the first.

What I keep returning to is how small the thing becomes once it has edges. The worst case, looked at plainly, usually has walls and a floor and a Tuesday in it. You can live inside something with edges.

Let's not confuse safety with a life, or security with being alive.
